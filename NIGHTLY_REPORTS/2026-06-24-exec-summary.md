## ELI5

Imagine you've already built a really good robot that helps make slideshows, and you
also gave it a "good taste" meter that scores how nice each slide looks. The whole robot
is finished. So tonight wasn't about building — it was about reading an old book of
"rules for what looks nice" and checking whether two of its rules were worth teaching the
robot.

The first rule said "shapes look best when they're sized like special magic rectangles."
The second rule said "use as few different sizes as possible." We looked carefully and
decided **not** to add either one. The first rule doesn't fit slides (our boxes are sized
to fit the words, not to be magic rectangles), and we couldn't even find the exact recipe
for it written down anywhere we trust. The second rule actually *fights* something good we
already do — slides are supposed to have a big title, medium body, and small caption (lots
of sizes on purpose!), and that rule would punish exactly that. So we wrote down *why* we
said no, and we changed no robot parts. We also double-checked that every auto-generated
list and chart in the project still matches, and they all did.

## Broad strokes

The product is complete and stable, so this was a careful "research and bookkeeping" night
rather than a building night. The standing job each session is to keep growing and
applying a small library of trustworthy research on how to measure good design with math
alone (no looking at pictures). We've already reached our target number of solid sources,
so the focus has shifted from *collecting* references to *deciding which ideas are actually
worth wiring in* — and, just as importantly, honestly recording the ones that aren't.

Tonight continued working through a single classic research paper that lists fourteen ways
to measure how a layout looks. Two of its ideas were examined this session: one about
preferred shape proportions, one about minimizing the number of different element sizes.
Both were declined, each for a clear reason written down in the project's notes — either
the precise math isn't reliably published anywhere, or the idea clashes with how good
slides are deliberately built. With these two, every measure from that paper that's worth
checking has now been checked, so we've formally closed that line of inquiry and noted that
future effort should go toward fine-tuning what's already built rather than mining the same
old paper.

The discipline here matters: it would be easy to bolt on more "fancy-sounding" scoring
rules to look busy, but adding a rule we can't justify would quietly make the good-taste
meter worse and less honest. Saying a careful, well-documented "no" protects the quality of
the finished product. Everything still passes its tests, every auto-generated document is in
sync, and the work is saved and backed up.
